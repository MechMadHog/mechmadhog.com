## Recovery Matters

Another sign that the weight has become excessive is the amount of recovery it now requires.

After carrying the full 18kg load, my back has needed significantly more recovery time. The muscles are constantly tight, sore, and demanding attention. I've needed extra stretching, mobility work, and rest days just to keep things functioning properly.

That's useful feedback.

Training is supposed to make you stronger, not leave you permanently recovering from the previous session.

If a training method starts interfering with recovery, it becomes harder to stay consistent. Consistency beats intensity over the long term. I'd rather complete hundreds of productive walks than spend weeks nursing an avoidable injury.

The goal is sustainable progress, not proving a point.

## Building the Back for Heavier Loads

One thing this experience has highlighted is that my cardiovascular system can handle the walks, but my back and supporting muscles need to catch up.

Fortunately, that's trainable.

The muscles that support loaded carries respond well to:

- One-arm dumbbell rows
- Shrugs
- Farmer's carries
- Lat pulldowns
- Seated rows
- Back extensions
- Sandbag carries

These exercises strengthen the upper back, traps, lats, spinal erectors, grip, and core... all of the muscles responsible for keeping the body stable under load.

Instead of forcing heavier rucks, it makes more sense to spend time strengthening the machinery that carries the weight. Once those muscles become stronger, heavier loads become safer and more comfortable naturally.

The solution isn't always more weight.

Sometimes the solution is becoming stronger.

## The Farmer's Walk Evolution

While 18kg may be excessive for an hour-long walk, it still has tremendous value in another role.

Farmer's carries.

One of the ideas I'm experimenting with is combining a loaded ruck with a dumbbell in each hand.

Weight on the back.

Weight in the hands.

Walking under load.

This turns a simple walk into a full-body strength exercise.

The grip works constantly.

The traps work constantly.

The upper back works constantly.

The core has to stabilise everything.

Even the legs and glutes are forced to contribute more.

Unlike many gym exercises, loaded carries train the body as a complete system rather than a collection of individual muscles.

## Why Rucking Works

Rucking is one of the most practical forms of conditioning I've ever used.

It increases calorie expenditure without requiring running.

It builds work capacity.

It improves posture awareness.

It develops mental resilience.

It can be adjusted simply by changing the load, distance, or pace.

Most importantly, it allows larger individuals to perform long-duration exercise without the joint impact associated with running.

For fat loss, it has been one of the most valuable tools in my arsenal.

## Why Farmer's Carries Work

If rucking is one of the best conditioning tools, farmer's carries are one of the best strength tools.

Farmer's carries develop:

- Grip strength
- Upper back strength
- Trap development
- Core stability
- Posture
- Work capacity
- Real-world strength

There is something fundamentally useful about picking up heavy objects and carrying them from one place to another.

No machine can truly replicate it.

It's simple.

It's brutal.

And it trains exactly what the human body evolved to do.

## The Lesson

Reaching the full 18kg ruck wasn't a failure.

It was useful information.

I've now found the point where the costs begin to outweigh the benefits.

That doesn't mean the weight was wasted.

It means I now know where my current limits are.

The smarter move is to use a weight that supports consistency, continue strengthening my back, and reserve the heavier loads for shorter carry-based training.

The goal isn't to carry the most weight.

The goal is to become stronger, leaner, healthier, and more capable.

Those aren't always the same thing.
